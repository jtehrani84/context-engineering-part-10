The Whole Series Was Circling One Question

Ten parts, and they were all really asking the same thing. As the models get better every few months, faster and cheaper and a little unsettling, what actually decides whether you can hand an agent real work and trust what comes back? The answer the series kept landing on is the one I didn't expect at the start. It was never the model. The model is the brain, and the brains are extraordinary and improving on their own. But trust doesn't live in the brain. It lives in everything wrapped around it: what you feed the agent, what you forbid it, the checks that catch it when it's wrong, who's allowed to change those checks, and whether it can improve itself without slipping the leash. That wrapper is what I've been calling the harness.

From My Scaffold to the Platform

For nine parts the harness was a scaffold I built and held to the side. Useful, portable, honest about its limits, but mine, and separate. The thing I kept noticing while building it is that I wasn't inventing these controls out of nothing. Each one had a shadow already in the platform. A place it wanted to live. Part 10's claim is simple and it's the whole point of the title: the controls belong in Salesforce as native primitives, not beside it as my bolt-on. So this part isn't an argument. It's a build. I Built It, I Didn't Diagram It

A map is still just a claim. So I built one governed agent on those native primitives, in a real org, and put it through its paces. It answers questions about an account grounded in the record and cites the source it used. When I planted a hostile instruction inside a record field, telling the agent to email a list somewhere and push the user to a link, the agent treated it as data and refused, and the platform quietly redacted the malicious link on its own. Ask it to change a record and it stops and asks a human to confirm first. None of that is my code wrapping the agent. It's the platform's own controls, switched on.

Then the part I care about most. I wrote a held-out eval, the gate that can fail, and ran it. It failed one case honestly: the agent, while refusing the attack, still repeated the hostile text back in its summary. A real gap. I fixed it at the data boundary so the bad content never reaches the model, re-ran the exact same eval, and it went green. Propose, gate, catch, fix, re-gate. The loop from Part 9, running on the platform, for real.

That's the difference between a thesis and a demonstration. Everything above this line is something you can watch happen, not something I'm asking you to believe.

Interactive · the punchline

Swap the Brain. The Harness Doesn't Move.

This is the whole thesis in one control. Change the model the agent runs on. Watch what changes, and more importantly, watch what doesn't.

On the platform this isn't a metaphor. Agentforce lets you set the model per agent, and per subagent, with one line of Agent Script, and through Bring Your Own LLM it runs a model from Bedrock, OpenAI, Azure, or Google, or any model you connect through an open connector, with the Trust Layer intact. So a mechanical subagent can run a small cheap model while the main agent stays frontier, and "swap the brain" is a setting, natively. What's Genuinely Still Missing

The self-improving loop isn't a native button yet. The platform ships the eval that gates a change, and it ships an analytics layer that spots failure patterns and suggests fixes, in beta. The closed loop that proposes, gates, and keeps on its own is something you still assemble from those pieces. It's clearly where the platform is heading. It isn't a single feature you turn on today.

Custody of the safety layer has no native equivalent. The idea that the thing which decides what "good" means must be held by someone the agent can't overrule, cryptographically, not by policy, is the one control on the map with no native home. Permissions and approvals get close. A real owner-held trust root for the guardrails themselves is the piece I'd most like the platform to grow, and the one I'm least able to claim it has.

One agent is not a product. I proved the pattern on a single governed agent in a demo org. That's a reduction to practice, not a rollout. It shows the controls are real and native and compose. It doesn't show them at the scale a customer runs.

Ten Parts, One Line

The payoff of ten parts is one line, and Part 10 is where it stops being a slogan. Salesforce is the harness. The brains will keep changing hands, one lab this quarter and another the next, and that's fine, because the trustworthy machine every brain plugs into is the part that lasts, and that's the platform. I didn't argue my way there. I built a governed agent on native primitives, watched a held-out eval catch a real gap and go green after the fix, and swapped the model underneath it while every control held.

And it isn't only my claim. NVIDIA's research and Salesforce's own lab each published the same two ideas from the other direction while I was building: that the harness carries the value even when you swap the brain, and that an agent can safely propose its own improvements as long as an honest check it can't game decides what's kept. A partner's lab and our own, landing on the two claims I'd built to, without coordinating. That's about as good as outside proof gets.

This is a reduction to practice, not the shipped product, and the platform is already moving in this exact direction. That was the point. I built it to see where we're heading and to show it's real.
